Notes from Polaroid: Information Asymmetry in a Motel Room

On directing a suspense short about a rigged dating experiment, and why I distrust software systems that hide the rules of the room from the people inside them.

Contents

  1. Why suspense on screen is a warning sign in software
  2. Designing the opposite of a thriller
  3. Agency requires seeing the seams

Filed under

#polaroid#filmmaking#ai#agency

Polaroid takes place inside the kind of room you can map in five seconds: a motel door, warm practical lamps, two people sitting across from each other for a dating experiment. Nothing explodes. Nobody chases a car down a freeway. All of the tension in the film comes from one structural fact established early on: one person in that room knows far more about the rules of the experiment than the other.

When you direct a two-person suspense scene in a single location, you learn quickly how little machinery is needed to make an audience uneasy. You do not need a villain twirling a mustache. You only need an uneven distribution of knowledge.

The person who knows the rules can afford to sound relaxed, curious, even generous. The person who does not know the rules has to spend half their attention answering the question on the table and the other half trying to infer what game is actually being played. Politeness stops feeling safe when the frame around the conversation is hidden.

Why suspense on screen is a warning sign in software

Over the past year, while moving between editing rooms and building software at Mind Light, I kept noticing that a huge amount of modern software puts human beings in the weaker chair of that motel room.

Sometimes the asymmetry is deliberate: pricing algorithms, dark patterns, or ranking feeds whose incentives run against the person scrolling through them. More often, especially in the wave of AI interfaces being bolted onto workplace and institutional tools, the asymmetry happens by accident because builders confuse a clean surface with an honest one.

Consider what happens when an AI assistant reads across an organization’s emails, financial rows, and grant documents, and presents a tidy paragraph or a pre-filled action button. On the surface, the interface feels helpful. It speaks in complete, confident sentences. Yet look at what the person sitting in front of the screen cannot see:

  1. Which underlying records were actually consulted, and which ones were missing or stale?
  2. Where did retrieved fact end and plausible-sounding inference begin?
  3. If the user clicks approve, what irreversible state changes or external messages leave the building?

When a tool hides those three answers behind a polished text box, fluency becomes a form of pressure. Questioning a confident summary takes time and social courage, especially for an overworked program manager or finance coordinator at the end of a long week. Slowly, without anyone signing a memo to give up authority, human review turns into a rubber stamp inside a room whose rules belong to the software.

Designing the opposite of a thriller

In cinema, my job in Polaroid was to sustain uncertainty until the truth of the room broke into the open. In software, my job is the exact opposite: collapse that uncertainty before anyone is asked to act.

An instant photograph, an actual Polaroid print, had a quality that digital generation quietly erased. Light from a real room hit chemical emulsion in front of the people who were standing there. You could misread a photograph’s context, of course, and Polaroid plays with how easily an image can be staged or weaponized. Still, the physical object carried a boundary: this frame came from a specific moment in a specific place.

Software handling real institutional stakes needs an equivalent discipline of provenance:

  • Show the receipts inline. If a system claims an obligation report is due on the fifteenth or that a donor household gave a certain amount last fiscal year, the citation to the underlying award or ledger row belongs right next to the sentence.
  • Keep silence legal. When the record is incomplete, the right answer from software is “the record does not show this,” never a smooth interpolation dressed up in the same font as verified facts.
  • Make drafts visibly provisional. Any AI-derived structure or proposed message should arrive as an editable draft with clear provenance, waiting for a named human who understands the consequences to inspect, revise, or reject it.

Agency requires seeing the seams

There is a temptation in product design to hide every seam so the machine feels magical. Magic is wonderful in a movie theater because you bought a ticket to be deceived for ninety minutes and walk back out into the parking lot afterward.

Nobody buys a ticket to be mystified by their accounting ledger, their grant compliance calendar, or the records of a community organization. When people carry real legal and moral responsibility for what happens next, magic is the wrong aesthetic. What they need is a well-lit room where both chairs face the same table, every rule is written in plain view, and the person accountable for the outcome holds the shutter in their own hand.
